Greece, three stops

A city, a real Greek island town, and a week of not moving. Three places over about two weeks, so nobody is packing every other day, with boat days for whoever wants them and a beach or a town for whoever doesn't.

Stop 1The cityAthens 3–4 nights

The Acropolis, the rooftops, the food markets, dinner at nine. Arrive off the overnight flight and lose the jet lag somewhere worth losing it.

Stop 2The real GreeceOne island town, still to choose 3–5 nights

A working island with an actual town: restaurants, a bakery, a harbor with fishing boats, all on foot. Enough visitors that a big American group is normal, few enough that a coffee still costs what a coffee costs. Good beaches nearby. The town is the reason.

Stop 3The last weekNaxos or Kos, set by the island before it 5–6 nights

Somewhere to stop moving. One address that takes the whole group, with great views, a great pool, easy sea access and good food on site. A town at the door is a bonus. Boats and day trips going out from nearby matter more.

A local’s read · September 2026

Tassos recommended Syros and Naxos

Tassos is Josh’s friend in Athens. He has spent part of every summer of his life on one or two Greek islands, which is fifty years of comparing them against each other, and he likes the same things Josh does: food, then beaches, then architecture. He was sent the shortlist and the size of the group and nothing else.

If it were my family, I would go with Syros and Naxos. With twelve people and kids, that is the combination that makes the most sense, and getting there and between them is easy.

Tassos, Athens

Second choiceKos with Kalymnos: fly into Kos, twenty minutes across by boat. Properly authentic, very good seafood, but less charm than the first pair.
On MilosThe only one he argued against. Amazing beaches, but it has gotten expensive, the best spots are down rough dirt roads, and it connects awkwardly to Naxos.
Booking the boatsOne to two months ahead for twelve people in summer, through Ferryhopper, the site that sells tickets for all the Greek ferry lines in one place.
Cars on NaxosRent them. The best of Naxos is inland and the mountain villages are unreachable without.
When to goJune or September if there is a choice. In late July and August the wind picks up and disrupts the smaller boats, so do not let the dates slip later.
AthensHe offered to point us at the places to eat before we get there.

Stop 1 · the city · 3–4 nights

Athens

Athens is the first stop: the nonstop from Newark lands in the morning, so the first day is a long, slow one (check in, a swim, a walk, an early dinner) and then two or three full days before the islands. The city runs late and warm in summer, so dinner at nine on a rooftop is normal, and some of the best things to do with kids happen after dark.

Athens is the one stop with a real city in it: Plaka, the old town under the Acropolis, and Monastiraki next to it for shopping and ice cream, Psyrri for the evening, the Lycabettus funicular, a little cable railway up the hill, for the sunset photo, and the sea at Vouliagmeni, down the coast, on a hot afternoon. After-dark options are the point here; the beaches are a break from the heat.

Where to sleepTwo neighborhoods do the job. Plaka, under the Acropolis, is walkable to almost everything, and the prettiest and priciest of the two. Koukaki is the quieter residential side ten minutes further out, cheaper, with the metro and the Acropolis Museum at the end of the street. In either one the shapes available are the same: a block of family rooms in a hotel with a pool, two or three apartments in the same building, or one large house that sleeps everyone under one roof.
Stop 2 · the real Greece · 3–5 nights · one of three

The island town

The middle week is the one that makes this a Greek trip. Three islands can carry it. Tassos has a preference, further up this page, and the rest of the research does not settle it. What separates them is the town, how easy the beaches are, whether twelve of us fit at one address, and which third stop they connect to, because each island is locked to a different one. And on all three there is souvlaki, grilled chicken and chips for anyone who has had enough octopus.

Why not Mykonos, Santorini, Paros or IosThey are the islands everyone has heard of, and that is the problem: in summer they run on cruise crowds and Instagram, with menus and hotel rates priced for a different trip. Mykonos might as well be Miami Beach. Milos is the one island below that is heading the same way, and it is on the list anyway because the landscape has no substitute; the cost shows up in the price, which is the highest of the three. The other two have the same light and the same sea, real towns, better food, and prices that still make sense.
How these differ

Three islands, three different things

All three are places people live in year-round. Past that they are unalike in almost every way, and the three cards below are the shortest version of the difference.

Hans Peter Schaefer · Wikimedia Commons

Syros

The city

A marble square the width of a city block, an opera house, and a high street of pharmacies and hardware shops. A cafe culture that runs all year, and its own protected cheese. The beaches are small and on the far side of the island.

Travel photography

Milos

The landscape

An old volcano that blew its side out, so the rock runs white, pink and gray and there are about seventy beaches. Nothing else in Greece looks like it. It is also the most expensive week, and there is no town to walk out into at night.

Tomisti · Wikimedia Commons

Kalymnos

The port town

Bare limestone mountains, and a harbor with the largest small-boat fishing fleet in the eastern Mediterranean tied up in it. The best fish and the cheapest week. Plainer than the other two, and the beach coast and the town are half an hour apart.

Stop 2 · option 1 of 3

Syros

The grandest town of the lot, and a working city

Town at 9pm
Miaouli Square is a marble plaza the width of a city block, with the town hall along one side, cafe tables under the trees and a bandstand. It fills up in the evening and stays busy late. Above it, Ano Syros is a medieval Catholic village of stepped lanes too narrow for cars.
Cars
Not needed in town, useful for the beaches. Ermoupoli is walkable end to end; Galissas, Kini and Vari are village beaches on the west and south coasts, all served by bus.
Beaches
The weak spot. They are small, they are on the other side of the island, and none of them is at the door. This is a town stop first and a swimming stop second.
Lodging
Stay in Ermoupoli itself, ideally the Vaporia district just north of the main square, where the old shipowners' houses are. Syros is the only one of the three with a real market in whole houses big enough for a group, alongside the usual apartments. It books out early.
Getting on
Fly from Athens, 35 minutes on Sky Express, daily except Saturdays. On to Naxos it is a direct boat, about an hour and a quarter, most days in summer.
Who goes
More Greeks than most islands, because it is the capital of the Cyclades, the ring of islands southeast of Athens, and a working port with a year-round life of its own. It is firmly on the tourist map too, and in July you will know it. What is different is that Greeks are here as well, all year round.

The trade-off. You get the best evenings of the three and somewhere to walk to after dinner. You give up the beach at the door: they are small and on the far side of the island. And if the picture in your head is a whitewashed village above a cove, this is not that.

What Tassos says
Where to stayErmoupoli, the town itself. He did not name a hotel; his point was that Ermoupoli is a working town, so being in it is the whole idea. Three or four nights.
Where to goThe old mansions and the harbor, and the tavernas locals eat in. Galissas and Kini for swimming, both calm and easy. Kini again in the evening: fish places right on the water for sunset.

The capital of the Cyclades, and the only one of these islands that is a city. Nineteenth-century shipping money built Ermoupoli a marble square, a town hall by Ernst Ziller, the German-born architect who designed much of official nineteenth-century Athens, and a small opera house copied from La Scala, the Milan theater that was then the most famous opera house in Europe. Two hilltop churches, one Catholic and one Orthodox, face each other over the rooftops. It has never been a resort.

Stop 2 · option 2 of 3

Milos

The strangest landscape in Greece, and the most expensive week on the list

Town at 9pm
You get one or the other. Plaka, the hill town (Milos has its own Plaka, unrelated to the Athens one), is the best-looking place on the island, but it sits a kilometer above the sea and almost nothing up there has a pool. Pollonia, on the north coast, is a flat fishing village with a row of restaurants along the water, and it is where a group this size would sleep.
Cars
Recommended. Seven bus routes run out from Adamas and back, including one straight to Sarakiniko, but the popular ones run a few times a day and the quiet ones no more than four.
Beaches
About seventy of them, and they are the reason to come. Sarakiniko is white volcanic rock worn smooth; Firiplaka, Tsigrado, Provatas and Paliochori have sand. The boat day to Kleftiko, the sea caves on the south coast, is the trip everybody names.
Lodging
Stay in Pollonia, the village on the north coast. It is the one address on the island where a group this size can be in a village and on the water at once, and the shapes available are small hotels, apartments and studios, a good share of them with a pool. Plaka, the hill town, has almost nothing to rent and no pool anywhere in it. Milos is the most expensive of the three by a wide margin.
Getting on
Fly from Athens on Olympic Air or Sky Express, a short hop, several a day in summer. On to Naxos it is a direct boat, four sailings a week.
Who goes
It got popular fast and the prices followed. Americans and Instagram have both found it. Not Mykonos or Paros yet, but heading that way, and you will notice.

The trade-off. You get scenery nothing else on this list can match, and about seventy beaches to use it on. You pay for it twice: it is the most expensive of the three by a wide margin, and you cannot have both the hill town and the water. With twelve people you sleep at sea level in Pollonia and drive up to Plaka for an evening.

What Tassos says
His warningMilos is the one island he argued against for this trip. The beaches are amazing, but it has gotten expensive, the best spots are down rough dirt roads, and it connects awkwardly to Naxos.

The Venus de Milo was dug out of a field here in 1820 and is now in the Louvre. Milos has been mined and quarried for three thousand years, and it looks like nowhere else in the Aegean because of it. Adamas is the port and the bus hub. Plaka is the hilltop capital, with a castle above it left by the Venetians, who ran these islands for four hundred years before the Ottomans took them. Pollonia is a flat fishing village at the northeast tip, and it is the one address on the island where twelve people can be on the water and in a village at the same time.

Stop 2 · option 3 of 3

Kalymnos

A working port town, and the easiest place to get out on a boat yourselves

Town at 9pm
Pothia is a working Greek town: neoclassical and Italian-era, not whitewashed. Italy governed these islands from 1912 to 1943 and left the public buildings behind. More Greeks than visitors on the harbor front, and noisy with scooters, which every visitor account mentions. Telendos, the car-free islet across the strait, is the evening instead: one lane of tavernas and a two-euro boat every half hour until late.
Cars
Useful, not essential, if you base on the Myrties and Massouri strip. Pothia to Massouri is twenty to thirty minutes on narrow roads and the municipal bus runs it several times a day.
Beaches
Massouri and Myrties face Telendos across a narrow strait and the sunset behind it is the island's picture. Boats small enough to drive with no license at all, thirty horsepower, up to seven people, are the thing neither of the other two islands offers.
Lodging
The widest choice of the three, and the cheapest by a distance. Stay either in Pothia, the port town, or out at Massouri and Myrties on the west coast facing Telendos. Apartments with kitchens are the common shape here, and it is realistic to put the whole group at one address.
Getting on
Fly from Athens to Kos, several a day, then the boat across to Pothia, thirty to forty-five minutes, about eighteen a week. The same boat is how you get to the third stop.
Who goes
Climbers, from all over, spring through autumn, along with divers, walkers and people off boats. It is an island that works for a living and takes visitors on the side, which is most of the appeal. Almost no cruise traffic: under a thousand passengers landed there in the whole of 2025.

The trade-off. You get the cheapest week by a distance, the best fish on the page, and a boat you can drive yourselves. You give up prettiness: the towns are plainer than Syros or Naxos, and nowhere here has the tight web of old lanes you wander into after dinner. Telendos, the little island across the water, comes closest, and getting there is a ten-minute boat ride.

What Tassos says
His second choiceKos with Kalymnos was the pair he named after Syros and Naxos: fly into Kos, twenty minutes across by boat. He rates Kalymnos as properly authentic, with very good fish.

Bare limestone mountains dropping straight into the sea, a big amphitheater of a harbor town, and the sunset behind Telendos from the Massouri side, which is one of the best views on any island on this page. Nothing here is the white-cube look people picture when they think of a Greek island. It is a different-looking place. Known for sponge diving and world-class rock climbing; less known for having the largest small-boat fishing fleet in the eastern Mediterranean, which is why food-serious travelers go. Pothia, the main port, is a real working harbor with some grit at the quay.

Stop 3 · the last week · 5–6 nights · one of two

The third stop

Five or six nights of not moving, somewhere that takes the whole group, with a pool, the sea, food and boats going out from nearby. Two islands can do it and they do it very differently. Two shapes have worked for this group before. One big house: everyone under one roof, cooking and drinks our own, cheapest per family, and it only works if everybody commits to the same week. Family suites side by side: half board, meaning breakfast and dinner are included, or dinner credits, so nobody plans meals, a club for the kids, and families can come for three nights or six.

Naxos

The island town

The best sand in the Cyclades and a real town right behind it, so the swimming and the walking out for dinner are the same week and nobody gets driven anywhere. It is the busiest island on this page, and in early July you feel it.

Ikos Resorts

Kos

The resort

Flat, long and green, with its own airport and one resort built for a group this size: pools, a private beach, a club for the kids, meals handled. The trade is that a resort could be almost anywhere. The Greek part of the week starts when you walk out of it, and the village is ten minutes away.

Stop 3 · option 1 of 2

Naxos

A town with the best sand in the Cyclades in front of it
Herbert wie · Wikimedia Commons
Stop 3 · option 1 of 2

Naxos

A town with the best sand in the Cyclades in front of it

Who it fits. A resort is the easy answer for the last week and there is nothing wrong with one, except that you could be almost anywhere while you are inside it. Naxos is the version where the last week is still Greece: four kilometers of soft shallow sand, and a town behind it you walk into for dinner. It also has the widest choice of places to stay on this page, villas included. The trade-off is the crowd. Getting there: a direct boat from Syros most days in summer, about an hour and a quarter, or from Milos four times a week. From Athens it is a short nonstop flight.

The trade-off. You get the beach and a real town in the same place, so the swimming and the evening do not need a car between them, and you get the widest choice of villas on this page. You give up quiet: this is the busiest island here, and in early July you feel it.

What Tassos says
Where to stayA villa, and he expects one big enough for all twelve to be easy to find here. He did not name a village; the beaches below are where the sand is. Five or six nights.
Where to goBeaches first: Plaka, Agios Prokopios and Mikri Vigla, all long sand and shallow water. Then the food, because Naxos is farming country, so the local cheese, the potatoes and the meat. Then the Portara at sunset, a single giant marble doorway left standing on the headland from a temple that was never finished, plus the old town and the mountain villages, Halki and Apeiranthos. You need cars to reach them.

The largest island in the Cyclades and the most agriculturally serious island on this page, with long shallow sandy beaches, mountain villages, Venetian towers, and food producers you can visit.

Stop 3 · option 2 of 2

Kos

The resort island, done properly

Who it fits. For the whole group under one roof and nothing to organize: Ikos Aria at Kefalos puts pools, an organized sandy beach, kids' and teen clubs, a spa and a dozen restaurants at one address, ten minutes' walk from a real village. The trade-off: more package tourism than the small islands, the north coast catches the summer north wind, and Kefalos is 40 minutes from Kos town. Getting there: the boat from Kalymnos, thirty to forty-five minutes, about eighteen a week, landing seven kilometers from the airport. Home is a flight to Athens from that same airport.

The trade-off. You get one address that handles everything: pools, a beach, a club for the kids, meals, and nobody organizing anything for five days. You give up the sense of being on a Greek island while you are inside it. Kefalos village is ten minutes away on foot, and that is where you get it back.

Long, narrow and flat, with a busy resort coast in the north and a quieter, whitewashed end at Kefalos in the south. For a last week it does the job well: a real airport with several Athens flights a day, sandy organized beaches, two water parks, and the one resort on this list built for families at scale. The history is here too: Hippocrates was born on Kos, and the Asklepieion, the ancient healing sanctuary where he taught, is a short ride from town.
